\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

This book began as an attempt to relearn a mathematics degree, and it found a method. The
method was to build a computer-algebra engine --- the kind of program that simplifies, differentiates,
integrates and row-reduces --- and to refuse to add any rule to it that could not be stated as a
theorem and, where the theorem was true, proved in Lean~4. A rule that could not be proved was
allowed in only with a label that said so, and with the counterexample that showed why.

That discipline turned out to be a better teacher than any textbook. A textbook says the product
rule holds; the engine, told to prove it, asks under what hypothesis, and the answer --- that both
factors must be differentiable at the point, and that without it the rule turns a non-existent
derivative into $1$ --- is the mathematics that was forgotten. A textbook says $x/x = 1$; the
engine's proof fails at $x = 0$, and the reader relearns what a side condition is. A textbook
says $\sqrt 8 = 2\sqrt 2$; the engine cannot say it in that form, and the reason is a theorem about
well-founded orders that most degrees never reach.

\paragraph{What is in the book.} The first five parts are the mathematics, in roughly the order a
degree teaches it: exact arithmetic and polynomials; the laws of exponents, radicals, and the
exponential and logarithm; the derivative and its rules; antiderivatives, substitution and
integration by parts; systems of linear equations and echelon form; partial orders, lattices and
fixed points; the $\lambda$-calculus; and finally the theory of well-founded orders, which is what
makes a rewriting engine terminate without a step budget. Every chapter follows one shape. The
mathematics is re-derived from its definitions, with proofs written out. Then the engine's rule for
it is shown, as code. Then the theorem about that rule is stated in Lean, with its hypotheses, and
what the proof needed is explained. Then the notebook is asked to run it.

The sixth part onward is the engineering: how the engine is organized, why soundness is a fold
over the rewriter, how the termination ordering was found and what it cannot afford, how a
selection is traced back through a derivation, and the ledger of what is proved. A reader who
wants the mathematics can stop before it; a reader who wants to build something like it should
not.

\paragraph{The four labels.} Every rule the engine fires is reported with one of four statuses,
and the book uses the same four words throughout.

\begin{defbox}[The four statuses]
  \begin{description}[nosep]
    \item[\status{verified}] An unconditional theorem states that the output of the rule means
      what its input meant, in the semantics of the chapter (the integers, the reals, the
      rationals under a linear system, the textbook definition of a partial order).
    \item[\status{conditional}] The theorem needs a hypothesis, and the necessity of the
      hypothesis is itself proved by a counterexample. The notebook shows the condition.
    \item[\status{checked}] The step is a guess, and a later step verifies it by an independent
      computation whose own steps carry their own statuses.
    \item[\status{unverified}] No theorem yet. The note beside the label says what would be
      needed.
  \end{description}
\end{defbox}

\paragraph{Who this is for.} Someone who once did the mathematics, has perhaps met Lean, and wants
to know what they still know. Proofs on paper are written in full; Lean proofs are quoted where
the statement is the point and summarized where the tactics are. No Mathlib is needed to read the
engine's own theorems; where the real numbers enter, the book says which Mathlib fact was used
and why.

\paragraph{The code.} Everything quoted is from the repository at the commit stamped on the title
page. Engine files are under \file{engine/MathEngine/}; real-number proofs are under
\file{proofs/Proofs/}. Boxes labelled \emph{Try it} give cells to type into the notebook.
